The Nook Tablet (2011)

Barnes & Noble's $249 answer to the Kindle Fire — an OMAP4 tablet whose FCC filing still says “NOOKcolor” on the label.

What is this thing?

The Nook Tablet is Barnes & Noble's 7-inch tablet, announced November 7, 2011 and released November 17, 2011, starting at $249 for 16 GB. Wikipedia It was the bookseller's answer to the Kindle Fire: a media tablet built around the Nook bookstore, with a 1 GHz dual-core TI OMAP4 inside. iFixit news

Here's the wrinkle that makes this device interesting on paper: the FCC filing behind it (XHHBNTV250-A) labels the product “NOOKcolor” — even though contemporary reporting ties that filing to the newly announced Nook Tablet. The label and the launch don't match, and we preserve the mismatch rather than flatten it. FCC filing Adweek

Not yet on our bench

This device is not on our bench. We don't own one, we haven't opened one, and we haven't powered one on. The outside

A 7-inch, 1024×600 IPS display in a dark gray plastic body with a distinctive notched lower edge — the Nook design language, carried over from the Nook Color but thicker to hold the bigger battery. Wikipedia No camera on the back; the microSD slot sits under a cover.

The photo below is a period shot: a salesman demonstrating a Nook tablet in a Barnes & Noble store — the device in its natural habitat.

The board

Teardown coverage describes a single main board with the OMAP4 processor, Hynix RAM, and SanDisk storage, sitting beside a notably large battery — Liliputing's summary of the dissection leads with “big battery, lots of adhesive.” Liliputing SlashGear's teardown walkthrough notes the clean, uncluttered internal layout. SlashGear

The FCC's internal photos (public since January 2012) show the same single-board construction with shielded radio sections. FCC filing We have not photographed the board ourselves.

The government's file on this device

The filing behind this device is XHHBNTV250-A, registered to Barnes&Noble.com for model BNTV250-A. The grant is dated November 7, 2011 — announcement day — and the internal photos, external photos, and user manual became public on January 9, 2012. FCC filing Confirmed — straight from the filing index.

The filing lists 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi around 88 mW. The schematics, block diagram, and operational description show no public availability date — long- and short-term confidentiality was requested and granted. FCC filing

And the naming wrinkle, preserved: the filing's own paperwork calls this product “NOOKcolor” — the previous-generation device — while the press, the dates, and the model number all point to the Nook Tablet. Whether that's a paperwork shortcut or a reused filing, the public record doesn't say. We report both, and we don't pick a winner.

Debug and service modes

The user manual — a public exhibit in the FCC filing — documents the ordinary factory reset through the Settings menu. FCC filing

We found no manufacturer-published hardware debug documentation for this model, and we have not entered any mode on any Nook Tablet. Listed for orientation, not from experience.

Our posture on all of the above: we describe what's publicly documented, and we don't go knocking. No device has been probed, reflashed, or opened on our bench — there is nothing here but reading.

What public records show

The filing label doesn't match the launch. “NOOKcolor” on the paperwork, Nook Tablet in the stores, same week. Filings get reused and relabeled more often than the industry admits — this one just left the evidence in the open. FCC filing Adweek

It's a clean, battery-forward design. Teardown coverage keeps coming back to the same two observations: a big battery and a tidy single board — a media tablet built to be held for hours. Liliputing

Words to know

Every term this page uses, in plain English. No prior knowledge required.

OMAP
Texas Instruments' line of mobile processors — the 44xx series powered a generation of tablets.
PMIC
Power-management IC: the chip that makes all the different voltages the board needs from the battery.
Touchscreen controller
The chip that turns finger touches on the glass into coordinates the processor understands.
FCC ID
The ID the FCC assigns to each approved wireless product. Punch it into fccid.io and the whole public filing comes up.
Short-term confidentiality
A deal with the FCC: the company files photos and manuals, but the public can't see them for a set number of days.
Factory reset
Wiping the device back to out-of-box software through the Settings menu — the supported, documented way.
