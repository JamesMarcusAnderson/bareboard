The Tile Mate (2019)

A little square that shouts over Bluetooth so you can find your keys. We haven't opened one yet — but the FCC filing and an independent teardown already tell us what's inside.

What is this thing?

The Tile Mate is a Bluetooth item tracker: a small square tag you attach to keys, bags, or anything you lose. When you can't find it, you ring it from the Tile phone app and the tag plays a loud tone; when it's out of earshot, the app shows its last known location, and other Tile users' phones can anonymously relay its position back to you. The unit covered here is the T9001 — the 2019 generation, the one that brought back the user-replaceable battery after earlier sealed designs. EDN teardown

Tile essentially invented the mainstream Bluetooth-tracker category in the 2010s, years before Apple and Samsung shipped their own tags. This report covers the Tile tracker line through its most-documented generation, the 2019 Mate.

Not yet on our bench

We don't own a Tile Mate yet, so nothing here comes from our hands: no disassembly, no board photos of our own, no measurements, no boot logs. Everything below is built from public records — the FCC certification filing and an independent published teardown — with each claim linked to its source. When a unit lands on our bench, we'll verify the hardware firsthand and update this page. Until then, treat this as a paperwork autopsy: honest about what the documents prove and what they don't.

A short history

  1. 2013–2015Tile ships its first Bluetooth trackers and defines the category: a tag, a phone app, and a crowd of strangers' phones quietly relaying locations. TechCrunch
  2. October 2016Tile introduces a thinner, lighter Tile Mate — the first big redesign of the line. TechCrunch
  3. 2019The T9001 generation ships with a user-replaceable battery, answering the loudest complaint about the sealed earlier models. EDN teardown
  4. August 1, 2019The FCC grants equipment authorization for the T9001 under FCC ID 2ABXLT9001, grantee Tile, Inc. FCC filing
  5. January 28, 2020The filing's internal photos, external photos, and user manual become public after short-term confidentiality expires; schematics, block diagram, and operational description stay permanently confidential. FCC filing

The outside

A white square, roughly the size of a matchbook, with a hole in one corner for a key ring and a single button (the Tile logo) in the middle. Press the button and it rings your phone; ring it from the app and the tag plays its tone through a small speaker grille. We don't yet have our own photograph — the image below is a placeholder until a unit reaches our bench.

The board

No board photos of our own yet — but an independent teardown opened the 2019 Mate and documented what's on its single small PCB: a Nordic Bluetooth SoC, a Micro Analog Systems driver chip, a printed circuit-board trace antenna, a push button, a piezo transducer for the ring tone, and visible test points. One chip marking in that teardown — P8904 DDA9 — could not be identified by the teardown author, and we won't guess at it either. EDN teardown

The FCC filing's internal photos (public since January 2020) show the same single-board construction from the certification lab's bench. FCC filing

The government's file on this tracker

Before Tile could sell the T9001 in the United States, the FCC had to certify its Bluetooth radio. The filing under FCC ID 2ABXLT9001 (grantee Tile, Inc.) records a 2.402–2.48 GHz Bluetooth transmitter at 3 mW conducted output power, granted August 1, 2019. The internal photos, external photos, and user manual went public on January 28, 2020; the schematics, operational description, and block diagram are listed but permanently confidential — the standard trade this system makes. Debug and service modes

The published teardown notes visible test points on the board — the usual factory programming and test access for a Nordic-based design. We have not probed them, don't own the hardware, and publish no pinout: anything about those points beyond "they exist" would be guessing until a unit is on our bench. Our posture throughout: we listen, we don't knock. If a future bench unit reveals a serial console or debug header, we'll document what it says read-only — never bypasses, never credential or key extraction, never firmware redistribution.

What public records show

  • The T9001's radio is a standard Bluetooth Low Energy design at 3 mW conducted — a short-range personal-area device, not a long-range transmitter. FCC filing
  • The bill of materials is minimal: one BLE SoC, one piezo driver, a PCB antenna, and support parts — the teardown author notes the design "relies on software," with the Nordic chip doing nearly everything. EDN teardown
  • Location history lives in Tile's cloud service, not on the tag — the tag itself just advertises its Bluetooth identity. That architecture is why the hardware can be this simple.

Words to know

Every term this page uses, in plain English. No prior knowledge required.

Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE)
A power-sipping flavor of Bluetooth for small devices that mostly sleep and occasionally chirp.
System on a chip (SoC)
The whole computer — processor and radio — on one piece of silicon.
Piezo transducer
A component that turns electricity into sound (and vice versa) using a vibrating crystal — the tag's tiny loudspeaker.
PCB antenna
An antenna drawn as copper traces right on the circuit board — no separate part needed.
Test points
Bare copper pads on a board where the factory connects probes for programming and testing.
Conducted output power
The radio's transmit power measured at the chip's output, before the antenna — 3 mW here, very small.
FCC
The Federal Communications Commission — the U.S. agency that regulates anything that transmits radio. Every wireless gadget sold here has to pass its tests first.
FCC ID
The ID the FCC assigns to each approved wireless product. Punch it into fccid.io and the whole public filing comes up.
