The Autopsy Report · Device #031

The Kasa HS100, Version 1

The smart plug that put a phone-controlled switch on millions of outlets — two circuit boards, one relay, and an open-source notice hiding in the packaging. Public-records edition: we haven't opened one yet.

What is this thing?

The TP-Link Kasa HS100 is a Wi-Fi smart plug: it plugs into a wall outlet, your lamp or coffee maker plugs into it, and then you control that device from the Kasa phone app, on a schedule, or by voice through Alexa or Google Assistant. Version 1 is the 2016 original — the step-up HS110 model adds energy monitoring, which this one doesn't have. EE Times teardown User guide

The v1 hardware carries FCC ID TE7HS100 — v1 only. The version 2 uses a different ID (TE7HS100V2), so check the label if you're buying one secondhand. FCC filing

Whose box is this?

Not ours — not yet. No unit of this device is on our bench. We haven't bought one, opened one, photographed one's insides, or listened to one boot. Everything on this page comes from public records: the EE Times teardown, the user guide, the FCC filing record, and — because no freely-licensed photograph of this device exists — a placeholder card where the photo would go. When a unit lands on our bench, this report gets a second edition with our own photos, measurements, and boot log.

A note on mains voltage: this device switches wall power. Nothing on this page is installation advice. If you open or install one of these, that work belongs to someone qualified — we document hardware, we don't do your electrician's job.

A short history

  1. November 2015The FCC grant for TE7HS100 issues — the v1 hardware clears the government before it ever reaches a shelf. FCC filing
  2. 2016TP-Link ships the HS100 under its Kasa Smart sub-brand, bringing the smart plug to big-box stores. User guide
  3. January 2018EE Times publishes a full teardown: two PCBs, a GPL notice in the box, and a unit bought on sale for $19.99. EE Times teardown
  4. 2017 – 2018TP-Link ships the v2 hardware (FCC ID TE7HS100V2) with different silicon — this page covers v1 only. FCC filing

The outside

A compact white plug with a single control button on the side and a Wi-Fi status LED. One honest admission: no freely-licensed photograph of the HS100 exists on Wikimedia Commons that we could verify, so the card below is a placeholder until a unit reaches our bench. (Photos of the HS110 exist — that's the energy-monitoring sibling, a different device, and we won't show you one labeled as the other.)

The board

We haven't held this board ourselves, so this is the teardown's account, not ours. The EE Times teardown found two separate PCBs inside — the same split-brain construction as the Belkin WeMo switch it was compared against: a power PCB carrying the AC switching and AC-to-DC conversion, mated to a digital PCB below it through two six-terminal connectors. The shell fought back — the two halves are glued and screwed, and the teardown author resorted to a hammer and screwdriver-as-chisel to get in. EE Times teardown

The teardown's most interesting non-hardware find: a GPL notice in the box — TP-Link ships open-source license paperwork with the plug, which means open-source software runs inside it. That's a fact about the box contents, not an invitation: this page contains no firmware procedures of any kind. EE Times teardown

Under the microscope: the chips

There is no chip table on this page — and that's deliberate. The EE Times teardown's published excerpts describe the two-board construction but don't resolve the v1 chip markings in text we could verify, and the full EDN sister article was unreachable when we checked. Several part numbers circulate for this hardware, but without a verifiable public source naming them on a v1 board, we won't print them. (Note the trap: the v2 uses different silicon entirely, so v2 sources don't count.) The chip table goes in when a unit reaches our bench or a verifiable v1 teardown surfaces.

What the public record does establish about the v1 hardware:

  • FCC ID TE7HS100, grantee TP-Link Technologies Co., Ltd., grant November 26, 2015 — v1 only. FCC filing
  • Two-PCB construction — separate power and digital boards. EE Times teardown
  • GPL notice in the packaging — open-source software inside, per the box contents. EE Times teardown

We haven't — there is no unit on our bench, so there is no boot log, no serial capture, nothing. That section gets written the day one arrives.

What we found

The GPL notice is the find. Most smart plugs ship as black boxes in every sense; TP-Link put a GNU General Public License notice in the HS100's box, acknowledging open-source software inside. That's a small, honest piece of paper that most buyers never unfold — and it makes this plug one of the more auditable ones in its class, at least in principle. EE Times teardown

v1 and v2 are different devices wearing one name. The version 2 moved to different silicon under its own FCC ID — so every "HS100 teardown" claim needs a version attached before you trust it. This page is v1, TE7HS100, and says so everywhere it matters. FCC filing

Words to know

Every term this page uses, in plain English. No prior knowledge required.

Smart plug
A plug that goes between your outlet and your device, letting you switch the device by phone, schedule, or voice.
Mains power
The AC electricity from your wall outlet — the dangerous kind. Respect it.
Relay
An electrically-controlled switch: a small signal flips a bigger, beefier switch inside.
PCB
Printed circuit board — the fiberglass slab the chips live on.
GPL
The GNU General Public License — an open-source license that requires sharing source code. Finding its notice in the box means open-source software runs inside.
Boot log
The text a device prints as it starts up. Engineers read it to see what's inside without opening anything.
UART / serial console
A simple port that lets you watch a device narrate its own startup, line by line.
Firmware
The software baked into the device — the part that runs before anything else.
FCC
The Federal Communications Commission — the U.S. agency that regulates anything that transmits radio. Every wireless gadget sold here has to pass its tests first.
FCC ID
The ID the FCC assigns to each approved wireless product. The first few characters are the company's grantee code; the rest is the maker's model code. Punch it into fccid.io and the whole public filing comes up.
