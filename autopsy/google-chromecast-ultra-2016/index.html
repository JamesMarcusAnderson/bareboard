The Autopsy Report · Device #046

The Chromecast Ultra (2016)

Google’s first 4K dongle: a Marvell-powered disc that needed wall power — and an Ethernet-equipped brick — to push 4K. We haven’t opened one — here’s the public record.

What is this thing?

The Chromecast Ultra is Google’s 2016 streaming dongle: a disc-shaped Chromecast that added 4K resolution and HDR (HDR10 and Dolby Vision). Google released it on November 6, 2016 at $69. Unlike earlier Chromecasts, it can’t run off a TV’s USB port — it needs the included wall supply, which conveniently also carries an Ethernet port for wired 4K streaming.Wikipedia

Inside, public hardware records name a Marvell BG4CDP (dual Cortex-A53 at 1.25 GHz), 1 GB of Samsung RAM, Toshiba flash, and a Marvell 88W8887 wireless chip.TechInfoDepot WikiDevi

Not yet on our bench

Let's be direct: BareBoard has never had this device on our bench. We have not possessed it, opened it, photographed it, measured it, or logged it. We have not captured a boot log, probed a debug port, or run a single test. Everything on this page comes from public records — FCC filings, manufacturer documentation, and published teardowns — linked inline so you can check every claim.

The things only a bench can provide — our own photos, physical measurements, a boot log, board-level confirmation of chip markings, and the exact revision of whatever unit eventually reaches us — are all still ahead of us. Until then, this is a public-records autopsy, and we say so out loud.

A short history

  1. October 4, 2016FCC application for A4RNC2-6A5-D (“Internet Video Streaming Device”), Google LLC.FCC filing
  2. November 6, 2016Chromecast Ultra released at $69: 4K, HDR10 and Dolby Vision, Ethernet in the power supply.Wikipedia
  3. 2019Google bundles the Ultra with its Stadia controller in the Stadia Premiere Edition — the pairing in our photo.Wikipedia
  4. August 6, 2024Google discontinues the Chromecast line, replacing it with the Google TV Streamer.Wikipedia

The outside

A black disc with the “G” logo and a short attached HDMI cable — the same silhouette as the second-generation Chromecast. Micro-USB handles power, and EDN’s teardown photos show a reset switch and a status LED beside the power input.Wikipedia EDN

The board

EDN’s teardown found two RF shields, generous thermal compound — the Ultra ran hot, and EDN’s writeup says so — plus a detachable internal HDMI connection, and the reset switch and LED next to the micro-USB input.EDN

Service and debug modes

The reset switch and status LED are the documented service features — visible in EDN’s teardown photos next to the micro-USB input. We found no publicly documented serial console, JTAG, or SWD interface — we listen, we don’t knock.

What the public record tells us

  • Wall power became mandatory. 4K’s appetite ended the era of TV-USB-powered Chromecasts — and Google turned the required brick into an Ethernet adapter.Wikipedia
  • A hot-running design. EDN’s teardown found two shields and heavy thermal compound — the Ultra worked hard for its 4K.EDN

Words to know

RF shield
A metal can soldered over the radio section — it keeps the device’s own signals from leaking out and outside noise from leaking in. Two shields means the radio layout was taken seriously.
Thermal compound
The paste between a hot chip and its heatsink — generous helpings mean the chip needed the help.
System on a chip (SoC)
The whole computer — processor, graphics, and I/O — on one piece of silicon.
FCC
The Federal Communications Commission — the U.S. agency that regulates anything that transmits radio. Every wireless gadget sold here has to pass its tests first.
FCC ID
The ID the FCC assigns to each approved wireless product. The first few characters are the company's grantee code; the rest is the maker's model code. Punch it into fccid.io and the whole public filing comes up.
Teardown
A published disassembly of a device, usually with photos and chip identification — someone else's bench work we can learn from.
UART / serial console
A simple port that lets you watch a device narrate its own startup, line by line. We listen; we don't knock.
JTAG / SWD
Deep debug ports wired straight into the chip — powerful, and easy to break things with. We map them from public records and leave them alone.
Firmware
The software baked into the device — the part that runs before anything else.
eMMC / NAND flash
The chip that holds the device's software when the power is off.
LPDDR
Low-power DDR memory — the short-term workspace chips in phones, tablets, and streamers.
