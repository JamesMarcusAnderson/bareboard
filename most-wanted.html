    The BB Ten Most Wanted

    The bounty board. These are the devices we most want on the teardown bench — ranked not by fame, but by how badly they need examining and how hard they are to find. When a device is acquired, it graduates off the board and the next one moves up.

    Most requested by visitors

    These are the devices our visitors look up most. When a device gets enough attention, it jumps the queue for a full teardown.

    1. Nintendo Switch — Joy-Con drift, Tegra X1, most-visited device hub
    2. Sony PlayStation — PSN breach, OtherOS lawsuit, repair restrictions
    3. Raspberry Pi — board revisions, Broadcom silicon, supply chain
    4. ESP32 — the maker’s microcontroller, open documentation
    5. TI-84 Plus — the calculator monopoly, DMCA battle
    6. Xfinity XB3 — teardown complete

    Rankings update as visitor analytics come in. The more a device gets looked up, the sooner it gets opened.

    The ranking

    50%Rarity. How hard is it to find? Used-market scarcity, active listing counts, discontinued with no new stock. The harder to source, the higher the bounty.
    30%Darkness. How little public research exists? No teardown, no FCC internal photos, no public documentation. Zero prior examination outranks everything except rarity itself.
    20%Reach. Installed base × still in use. A rare device nobody owned matters less than a rare device millions once owned.

    Score = 0.5 × Rarity + 0.3 × Darkness + 0.2 × Reach, each scored 0–10. Recomputed whenever the device universe updates or a bounty is claimed.

    The board

    1. Humane AI Pin High priority
      Failed 2024 startup device, now disappearing from the market. Snapdragon-based wearable with almost no public board-level documentation. Why wanted: documents an entire product category’s rise and fall at the silicon level.
    2. Rabbit R1 High priority
      MediaTek-powered AI handheld. Little verified teardown data exists on its internals, power design, or component choices. Why wanted: independent examination of heavily marketed AI hardware.
    3. Apple Vision Pro High priority
      Dual-chip (M2 + R1) spatial computer. Apple publishes nothing about the board; independent analysis is scarce and the display stack is unique. Why wanted: the most complex consumer board of its generation, undocumented.
    4. Nintendo Switch 2 High priority
      New generation hardware with unknown silicon revisions, board layout, and repairability characteristics. Why wanted: baseline documentation before revisions and cost-downs change the design.
    5. Meta Quest Pro Elevated
      Discontinued headset with pancake-lens optics and Snapdragon XR2+ board. Units are vanishing; the display assembly deserves recording. Why wanted: preservation of a short-lived premium design.
    6. DJI Avata 2 Elevated
      FPV drone with OcuSync video pipeline and tight firmware lockdown. Why wanted: independent examination of video transmission hardware the manufacturer keeps closed.
    7. Amazon Astro Elevated
      Home robot, rare in the wild, with an unknown sensor and compute stack. Why wanted: almost no public hardware analysis of a shipping home robot.
    8. Oura Ring 4 Elevated
      Extremely compact wearable; no public teardown of the current generation. Why wanted: miniaturization techniques worth studying and recording.
    9. Peloton Bike+ Standard
      Subscription-linked exercise hardware. Why wanted: documents the “connected hardware” category where software decisions can limit hardware you own.
    10. Steam Deck OLED Standard
      Valve handheld with some existing coverage. Why wanted: the HDR OLED panel supply chain and revised board layout deserve a complete record.

    Have one of these on a shelf? Suggest a device or offer hardware — every bounty claimed is documented and the board updates.

    BareBoard Legal Framework & Declarations

    • No AI training: All text, findings, and original photography on this site are copyrighted. Automated scraping for AI model training is prohibited. Crawlers that ignore robots.txt are trespassing.
    • Educational nature: BareBoard operates exclusively as a non-commercial, open educational resource. All material is compiled for academic evaluation, hardware preservation, and technical literacy under 17 U.S.C. § 107 (fair use).
    • Zero-leak / no-binary clause: This site does not host, distribute, or mirror proprietary firmware binaries, encrypted operating system keys, or weaponizable exploits. Documentation covers only the public, physical realities of circuit hardware.
    • Property verification: Every hardware autopsy is performed on personally owned, legally acquired equipment. No rented or third-party infrastructure is used.
    • Public source sourcing: Comparative and regulatory data is aggregated from official public filings, including the U.S. Federal Communications Commission equipment authorization database.
